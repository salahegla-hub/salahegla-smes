## Abstract

This brief examines how **social innovation** was used as an instrument of equality-oriented education policy in Finland, where the policy vision rested on sustained, sequential packages of reform rather than a single intervention.

## The six reforms

1. **Free education** — from pre-primary through higher education, including books, a daily meal, and transport for pupils living far away.
2. **Curriculum as process, not product** — flexibility built on central leadership with local administration, and curriculum review roughly every ten years.
3. **Phenomenon-based learning** — starting from a phenomenon to grasp its dimensions before going deeper into the relevant fields, under the concepts of the "learning community" and participatory education.
4. **Assessment reform** — a school system not based on grades; assessment rests on a pupil's capacity to learn and the paths available to improve, with grading prohibited by law.
5. **A culture of responsibility and trust** — most teachers hold a master's degree and have research experience, and Finland invests **thirty times** more in the professional development of teachers and administrators than in assessing pupil performance and testing.
6. **Special-needs education** — most such pupils attend state schools, with funding for those who assist their comprehension.

## Results

Before reform, Finland ranked poorly, with a low-quality system heavy with bureaucracy and inequality. Afterwards:

- **First** among OECD countries' schools
- More than **99%** of pupils complete compulsory basic education, and **90%** upper secondary
- More than **50%** of older adults participate in adult education programmes
- Government covers **98%** of the cost of education
- The **smallest gap in the world** between the highest- and lowest-achieving pupils

## Success factors and sustainability

A focus on pupils' needs; deep teacher training; trust-based professionalism that delegates curriculum judgement to teachers and principals; flexibility and interaction between community, government and schools; linking education to the labour market; and differentiated, adaptive curricula.

What distinguishes the Finnish system is that it was **designed to be sustainable and adaptive** to changes such as migration, an ageing population, shifts in the labour map and unemployment, and the emergence of new kinds of work.

## Recommendation

Social innovation in schools helps reduce educational disparities; in the Finnish case it played a guiding role in reforming the system to become more inclusive and equal.
